\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the empirical findings from the v31 estimation pipeline. The results are organised around the two research questions stated in Chapter~\ref{sec:intro_rqs}. RQ1 asks whether central bank tone explains announcement-window financial market reactions after controlling for the contemporaneous monetary policy surprise. RQ2 asks whether central bank language has predictive content for near-term policy outcomes and two-year yield changes in a strictly expanding-window setting.

The chapter proceeds in five steps. Section~\ref{sec:res_scores} describes the behaviour of the three tone measures: the dictionary score, the Random Forest score, and the RoBERTa/BERT sentence-aggregation score. Section~\ref{sec:res_agreement_new} compares the classifications produced by the methods and by the policy benchmarks. Section~\ref{sec:res_rf_new} reports the Random Forest classification evidence and interprets the main data-driven features. Section~\ref{sec:res_rq1_new} presents the market-reaction results for RQ1. Section~\ref{sec:res_rq2_new} presents the expanding-window forecasting evidence for RQ2. The final section summarises the empirical answer to both research questions.

Two points should be kept in mind when reading the chapter. First, the Random Forest classification exercise in RQ1 is descriptive: the model is trained to classify the sign of the contemporaneous rate decision and the continuous \rfscore{} is then used as a text-derived tone measure. Its high agreement with the rate decision is therefore not an independent validation test, although out-of-bag predictions reduce mechanical in-sample overfitting. Second, the RQ2 results are the true forecasting exercise: the model is refit only on information available before the forecasted meeting, and the prediction is evaluated on the next observation in chronological order.

\section{Tone Scores Across Corpora}
\label{sec:res_scores}

The final corpus contains 884 documents split between FOMC statements, FOMC press conferences, ECB policy statements, and ECB press conferences. The dictionary score is available for all documents. The Random Forest and RoBERTa/BERT scores are available for slightly smaller samples because model training requires non-missing rate-decision labels and the sentence-level classifier is only merged where document-level sentence predictions are present.

\Cref{tab:score_summary_v31} gives the basic distributional evidence. The dictionary score is centred close to zero in all four corpora, but the dispersion is much larger in press conferences than in statements. This is consistent with the institutional structure of the documents. Statements are short and formulaic, while press conferences contain longer explanations, conditional language, and discussion of risks. The RoBERTa/BERT score is more dovish on average in all four corpora, especially for FOMC press conferences, where the mean score is $-0.229$. The Random Forest score is close to zero on average, reflecting its construction as $P(\text{hike}) - P(\text{cut})$.

The label distributions confirm that the three methods do not simply reproduce the same classification rule. In FOMC statements, the dictionary classifies 94 documents as hawkish, 36 as neutral, and 112 as dovish. The Random Forest classifies the same corpus much more neutrally, with 133 neutral labels, while RoBERTa/BERT assigns 149 dovish labels. A similar contrast appears in FOMC press conferences: the dictionary is relatively balanced between hawkish and dovish classifications, whereas RoBERTa/BERT classifies 81 of 91 scored press conferences as dovish. For ECB statements, all methods are more neutral because ECB policy decision press releases are shorter and more formulaic. In ECB press conferences, the dictionary again produces a large hawkish--dovish split, while RoBERTa/BERT is more dovish and the Random Forest is mostly neutral.

This pattern has a direct interpretation for the later results. The dictionary is a transparent stance measure that reacts to explicit policy-action, inflation, employment, and risk phrases. RoBERTa/BERT is more sensitive to sentence-level semantic cues, including accommodative or risk-management language. The Random Forest is closest to the realised rate-decision benchmark, because it is trained on the decision sign. The methods therefore capture related but not identical objects: explicit textual stance, sentence-level semantic stance, and decision-predictive language.

Agreement with the rate-decision benchmark differs sharply across methods. The Random Forest agrees with the realised rate decision in 84.3\% of FOMC statements, 94.5\% of FOMC press conferences, 88.7\% of ECB statements, and 89.6\% of ECB press conferences. This result shows that the bigram-based model successfully recovers language associated with cuts, holds, and hikes, but it is not a pure out-of-sample forecasting result. The dictionary has much lower rate-decision agreement, especially in press conferences. This is not necessarily a failure of the dictionary: the dictionary is designed to measure the stance of the communication, not only the mechanical direction of the current rate decision. A central bank can hold rates while delivering hawkish forward guidance, or cut rates while communicating that no further accommodation is expected.

Agreement with the MP1/OIS\_MP1 surprise benchmark is lower than agreement with the rate decision. This is expected. MP1 measures the unexpected component of the policy action in a narrow event window, whereas the tone scores measure the content of the communication. A statement can be hawkish in tone even if the target-rate surprise is close to zero, and a rate surprise can be large even if the accompanying language is neutral. The evidence therefore supports using MP1/OIS\_MP1 as a control rather than as the object that text scores should mechanically reproduce.

\label{sec:res_rf_new}

Balanced accuracy is also high, which indicates that the model is not only learning the majority hold class. The feature-importance results are economically interpretable. For FOMC statements, hawkish directional features include terms such as ``short term'', ``economic expansion'', ``policy remain'', ``maintain price'', and ``ongoing increases''. Dovish features include ``economic weak'', ``generate economic'', ``auction facility'', and ``discount action''. For ECB press conferences, the most important hawkish terms include ``rate increase'', ``persistent upward'', and ``warrant continue'', while dovish terms include ``rate cut'', ``deposit facility'', ``facility rate'', and ``gradual recovery''. These terms are not merely generic sentiment words. They map closely onto monetary-policy language about rate changes, inflation pressure, policy accommodation, and the macroeconomic outlook.

The feature evidence also reveals why the Random Forest is useful as a complement to the dictionary. Some selected terms overlap with the hand-built dictionary, such as rate increases, cuts, upward pressure, and downside risks. Others are more institutional or contextual, such as terms connected to facilities, reserve operations, or recurring central bank formulations. The blocklist removes many speaker-name and institution-name artifacts, but the remaining terms still partly reflect the institutional language of each central bank. This reinforces the interpretation of the Random Forest as a data-driven communication classifier rather than a purely structural measure of economic tone.

The main finding is that text contains some market-relevant information, but the evidence is concentrated in specific asset classes and methods rather than being uniform across all outcomes. For FOMC statements, explanatory power is strongest for the two-year Treasury yield. The common-sample $R^2$ is 0.2408 for the dictionary, 0.2510 for the Random Forest, 0.2521 for RoBERTa/BERT, and 0.2619 in the full horse race. The corresponding coefficient evidence shows that the Random Forest and RoBERTa/BERT scores have statistically meaningful positive associations with the two-year yield: the RF coefficient is 0.0166 with $p=0.030$, and the RoBERTa/BERT coefficient is 0.0376 with $p=0.020$. In the joint horse race, the RoBERTa/BERT coefficient remains marginally significant for the two-year yield ($p=0.067$), while the dictionary and RF scores do not independently survive once all three are included.

For FOMC equities and foreign exchange, text adds little beyond the rate-surprise control. The S\&P 500 models all have $R^2$ values close to 0.067, and none of the score coefficients is statistically significant. EUR/USD behaves similarly. This weak equity and FX evidence is consistent with two offsetting channels. A hawkish statement can raise discount rates, which is negative for equities, but it can also signal stronger economic conditions, which is positive. In the exchange-rate regressions, the sign also depends on whether markets interpret hawkish language primarily as a rate-path signal or as an information signal.

For ECB statements, the strongest market-reaction result is in equities. The Random Forest model raises the SX5E $R^2$ from 0.1800 for the dictionary model to 0.2084, and the RF coefficient is positive and statistically significant ($\hat{\beta}=0.3713$, $p=0.028$). In the joint horse race, the RF coefficient remains significant at the 5\% level ($p=0.045$). The positive sign is consistent with an information-effect interpretation: ECB language associated with tighter policy decisions is also language associated with stronger macroeconomic conditions, and equity markets respond positively to that information. For OIS\_MP2, RoBERTa/BERT performs best in $R^2$ terms, but the coefficient is only marginal in the single-score model ($p=0.105$) and does not survive the full horse race at conventional levels.

The evidence for ten-year yields is weak. The ECB OIS 10Y regressions have low explanatory power, partly because the common sample is much smaller ($N=113$) than for the other ECB outcomes. FOMC ten-year Treasury reactions show slightly higher $R^2$ under RoBERTa/BERT than under the dictionary, but the score coefficient is not significant at the 10\% level in the baseline single-score model. This maturity pattern is intuitive. Textual policy tone should primarily affect short and intermediate maturities, where expected near-term policy rates dominate, while ten-year rates also embed term premia, long-run inflation expectations, and global risk factors.

\subsection{Press Conference Evidence}
\label{sec:res_pc_new}

Press conferences provide a useful robustness check because they contain longer explanations and answers to questions. The evidence is stronger in some press conference regressions but also less clean because the FOMC press conference sample is short and starts only in 2011.

For FOMC press conferences, the Random Forest score is significantly associated with EUR/USD, the two-year Treasury yield, and the ten-year Treasury yield. The RF coefficient is 0.2018 for EUR/USD ($p=0.025$), $-0.0395$ for UST 2Y ($p=0.003$), and $-0.0196$ for UST 10Y ($p=0.036$). The negative yield signs are not the standard pure policy-tightening prediction. A plausible interpretation is that post-2011 press conferences often combine discussion of tightening conditions with risk-management or forward-guidance explanations, so the RF score may be capturing broader decision-predictive language rather than a clean hawkish yield shock. RoBERTa/BERT is marginally significant for FOMC press conference EUR/USD and yields, but these effects weaken in the joint horse race.

For ECB press conferences, RoBERTa/BERT is the clearest equity signal. The coefficient on \bertscore{} in the SX5E regression is 0.4554 with $p=0.015$. The Random Forest score is marginally associated with EUR/USD in the single-score model ($\hat{\beta}=-0.1593$, $p=0.052$) and significant in the horse race ($p=0.031$). RoBERTa/BERT also contributes to the OIS\_MP2 horse race, where its coefficient is positive and significant ($p=0.015$). The overall pattern again suggests that press conferences contain richer semantic information than statements, but the mapping from tone to market reaction is less stable because press conferences mix policy explanation, macroeconomic assessment, and risk-management language.

The decomposition adds modest explanatory power for FOMC outcomes and larger explanatory power for ECB yield outcomes. Aggregating them into a single net hawkish--dovish score therefore hides useful variation.

\subsection{Fixed Effects and Forward-Looking Language}
\label{sec:res_fe_fwd_new}

The fixed-effect ladder supports the main interpretation. The strongest statement-level Random Forest result, FOMC UST 2Y, is stable across the base model, chair fixed effects, and chair-plus-era fixed effects. The coefficient is 0.0166 in the base specification, 0.0175 with chair fixed effects, and 0.0176 with both chair and era fixed effects; all three estimates are significant at the 5\% level. The ECB SX5E Random Forest result is also stable in magnitude: the coefficient increases from 0.3713 in the base specification to 0.4094 with chair fixed effects and 0.4228 with chair and era fixed effects, although the final specification is only marginal ($p=0.102$).

The forward/backward decomposition is more mixed. In FOMC statements, the forward-looking dictionary score is negatively associated with S\&P 500 returns in the base specification, but the result disappears after chair and era fixed effects are added. In FOMC press conferences, forward-looking dictionary tone is negatively associated with Treasury yields: the UST 10Y coefficient is approximately $-0.073$ to $-0.075$ across the three fixed-effect specifications and remains significant at the 5\% level. This is the clearest evidence that forward-looking language in press conferences contains information not captured by the aggregate score, although the sign again points toward an information or risk-management interpretation rather than a simple tightening channel.

For ECB statements, the forward/current split is strongest in equities. Forward-looking tone has a negative coefficient in the SX5E regression, while current-condition tone has a positive coefficient. Both effects are significant in the base specification and remain economically similar with fixed effects. This sign reversal is important. It suggests that forward-looking hawkish language may be interpreted as future tightening, which is negative for equities, while current-condition hawkish language may be interpreted as evidence of stronger economic conditions, which is positive for equities.

The first RQ2 finding is that language helps forecast the direction and size of future rate decisions, but it does not reliably forecast future two-year yield changes. For RF forecasts, direct out-of-sample $R^2$ is positive for all rate-decision targets. It is largest for FOMC statements, where the model reaches 0.304 for the next decision and 0.174 for the two-meeting-ahead decision. It is smaller for the ECB, especially in press conferences, but remains positive. In contrast, all RF two-year yield forecasts have negative direct out-of-sample $R^2$. The RF therefore captures persistent decision-language patterns but does not translate them into accurate forecasts of future two-year yield movements.

The RoBERTa/BERT forecasts strengthen the rate-decision result. The direct out-of-sample $R^2$ values are positive for all rate-decision targets and exceed the RF values in each corpus. For FOMC statements, RoBERTa/BERT reaches 0.457 for the next decision and 0.298 for the two-meeting-ahead decision. For ECB press conferences, the corresponding values are 0.281 and 0.226. However, RoBERTa/BERT also fails to deliver robust direct forecasts of two-year yield changes: the yield $R^2$ values are mostly close to zero and remain negative in the direct forecast evaluation.

For FOMC press conferences, adding the RoBERTa/BERT next-decision forecast raises $R^2$ by 10.5 percentage points relative to the inertia baseline, and the forecast coefficient is significant at the 1\% level. For ECB press conferences, the corresponding increment is 4.9 percentage points, also significant at the 1\% level. This suggests that sentence-level semantic tone is particularly useful in longer explanatory documents, where central bankers discuss the likely future path of policy more explicitly than in short statements.

The yield-forecast results require a more cautious interpretation. Some regression-based incremental $R^2$ values are positive, especially for RoBERTa/BERT in ECB yield specifications. However, the direct out-of-sample $R^2$ values in \Cref{tab:rq2_direct_oos_v31} are negative for yield targets. The conservative conclusion is therefore that central bank language predicts the next policy decision better than it predicts subsequent two-year yield changes. This distinction is economically plausible. Future rate decisions are partly governed by central bank communication and policy inertia, whereas future yield changes also reflect incoming macroeconomic data, risk premia, market positioning, and global shocks between meetings.

\section{Synthesis of Results}
\label{sec:res_synthesis_new}

The empirical results support three central conclusions.

First, central bank language contains measurable information, but the type of information depends on the scoring method. The dictionary and RoBERTa/BERT scores agree strongly for statements, especially ECB statements, but diverge substantially in press conferences. The Random Forest is closest to the contemporaneous rate-decision benchmark because it is trained on that object. This is useful for classification but should not be interpreted as independent evidence that the Random Forest discovers the true economic stance of communication.

Second, the answer to RQ1 is conditional rather than universal. Textual tone explains some announcement-window market reactions after controlling for rate surprises, but the effects are concentrated. The strongest statement-level evidence is for FOMC two-year yields and ECB equities. For FOMC statements, RF and RoBERTa/BERT scores are positively associated with UST 2Y reactions. For ECB statements, the RF score is positively associated with SX5E reactions, consistent with an information-effect interpretation. Equities and exchange rates do not show a stable dictionary-based relationship across corpora. Ten-year yield evidence is weak, which is consistent with the idea that long yields are less tightly linked to near-term communication tone.

Third, the answer to RQ2 is stronger for future policy decisions than for future yields. Both RF and RoBERTa/BERT produce positive direct expanding-window $R^2$ values for next-meeting and two-meeting-ahead rate decisions. The same is not true for two-year yield changes, where direct out-of-sample performance is mostly negative. This implies that communication helps forecast the central bank's own subsequent policy actions, but it does not reliably forecast the market's subsequent yield adjustment.

Overall, the results favour a layered interpretation of central bank communication. Dictionary scores provide transparent and theoretically structured measures. Random Forest scores capture decision-predictive language that is useful for classification and some market-reaction regressions. RoBERTa/BERT scores capture semantic information that is especially useful in press conferences and in expanding-window rate-decision forecasts. The methods are therefore complementary, but their economic interpretation differs. The evidence is strongest when the outcome is close to the communication object itself---the next policy decision or short-maturity yields---and weaker when the outcome is more exposed to competing macro-financial forces.
