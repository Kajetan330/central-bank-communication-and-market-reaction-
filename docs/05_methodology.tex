This chapter details the methods used to measure the tone of central
bank communication and to estimate its association with financial
market outcomes and future rate decisions. The chapter is organised
around the three-pipeline structure illustrated in
\Cref{fig:framework}: a hand-crafted dictionary
(\Cref{sec:meth_dictionary}), a data-driven Random Forest
(\Cref{sec:meth_rf}), and a fine-tuned RoBERTa sentence-level
classifier (\Cref{sec:meth_bert}) run in parallel on the same
cleaned corpus, each producing a continuous score and a discrete
label. \Cref{sec:meth_regressions} specifies the common econometric
framework (market-reaction regressions for RQ1, next-rate prediction
for RQ2, and the comparison tests) into which all three score
streams feed.

Before turning to the technical details, \Cref{fig:framework}
summarises the analysis framework. A hand-crafted Dictionary, a
data-driven Random Forest, and a fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier run
as parallel pipelines on the same cleaned document corpus, each
producing a continuous score and a discrete label. All three score
streams enter a common econometric framework (market-reaction
regressions for RQ1 and next-rate prediction for RQ2) so that the
three methods can be compared directly on identical outcome
variables and identical samples.

\section{Dictionary-Based Tone Scoring}
\label{sec:meth_dictionary}

\subsection{Design and Term Selection}
\label{sec:meth_dict_motivation}
\label{sec:meth_dict_selection}

The dictionary approach rests on the premise that the hawkish--dovish
stance of a central bank communication can be inferred from the
relative frequency of stance-bearing terms. This premise is
supported by the empirical finding that even simple word-counting
methods capture economically meaningful information from financial
texts \citep{tetlock2007}, provided that the vocabulary is
domain-specific rather than drawn from a general-purpose sentiment
lexicon \citep{loughran2011}.

Prior monetary policy dictionaries have taken two approaches.
\citet{lucca2009} compute the ``semantic orientation'' of FOMC
statements by measuring their linguistic proximity to hawkish and
dovish anchor texts, effectively treating the entire document as the
unit of comparison. \citet{picault2017} construct a more granular
ECB-specific dictionary that classifies individual terms as hawkish
or dovish, with a gradation system that distinguishes between terms
of different signal strength. \citet{apel2014} apply a similar
word-list approach to Riksbank minutes. The present dictionary
follows the term-level classification approach of
\citet{picault2017} and extends it in three ways: (i)~coverage of
both FOMC and ECB vocabulary in a single dictionary,
(ii)~systematic treatment of multi-word expressions (bigrams and
trigrams), and (iii)~organisation into four semantic categories
that mirror the dual-mandate reaction function.

The dictionary was constructed through an iterative, domain-informed
process. The initial term set (versions~14--15) was developed by
reading a sample of FOMC statements, ECB introductory statements,
and press conference transcripts, and identifying words and phrases
that appeared predominantly in communications associated with rate
hikes (hawkish) or rate cuts (dovish). Terms were retained only if
they satisfied two criteria: (i)~the term carries a clear
directional signal in the context of monetary policy communication
(not merely in general economic discourse), and (ii)~the term
appears with sufficient frequency across the sample to contribute
meaningfully to the score.

Version~34, used in this thesis, expanded the dictionary through
successive structured review rounds. Each candidate term was
evaluated against the two criteria above and either accepted,
rejected, or reclassified. Eight entries from version~33 were
pruned because they matched fewer than three documents across the
full corpus. The full dictionary is reported in
\Cref{app:dictionary}.

A distinctive feature of this dictionary is the systematic
inclusion of bigrams and trigrams alongside unigrams. Many
policy-relevant signals are inherently phrasal: ``rate hike'' is a
hawkish bigram, but neither ``rate'' nor ``hike'' in isolation
carries an unambiguous stance signal (``rate'' appears in neutral
contexts; ``hike'' rarely appears alone in central bank text).
Similarly, ``well above'' signals that inflation exceeds the
target, but ``well'' and ``above'' individually are uninformative.
The trigram ``decided to raise'' captures the precise moment of a
policy action that no unigram can convey.

To match multi-word expressions in the text, the scoring function
tokenises each document into unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams using
sliding windows. Each n-gram is Porter-stemmed (both sides of
bigrams and all three components of trigrams are stemmed
independently), and the stemmed n-grams are matched against the
pre-stemmed dictionary entries. This ensures that ``raising rates''
matches the dictionary entry ``raise rates'' (both stemming to
``rais rate'') and that ``decided to raise'' matches regardless of
whether it appears as ``decides to raise'' or ``has decided to
raise'' in the text.

The matching procedure counts occurrences independently at each
n-gram level: a sentence containing ``decided to raise rates'' will
generate one trigram match (``decided to raise'') \emph{and} one
bigram match (``raise rates'') \emph{and} one unigram match
(``raise''). This additive counting is intentional: a sentence that
uses multiple stance-bearing expressions at different n-gram levels
is indeed making a stronger stance signal than one that uses a
single expression.


\subsection{Semantic Categories and Dual-Mandate Decomposition}
\label{sec:meth_dict_categories}
\label{sec:meth_dual_mandate}

The dictionary terms are organised into four semantic categories
that reflect the structure of central bank communications and the
dual-mandate reaction function. Both FOMC statements and ECB
introductory statements typically follow a predictable template:
an assessment of current economic conditions (inflation, growth,
employment, financial conditions), followed by the policy decision
and a forward-looking paragraph on the expected path. The dictionary
categories mirror this structure. \Cref{fig:dict_arch} visualises
the architecture, and \Cref{tab:dict_counts} reports the realised
term counts.

The first category, \textbf{rate action}, captures the most direct
signals of policy stance: terms describing actual or intended rate
changes. Hawkish examples include ``decided to raise,'' ``rate
hike,'' ``point increase,'' ``further increases,'' and ``raise
rates.'' Dovish examples include ``to lower,'' ``point reduction,''
``decrease,'' and ``reduction.'' These terms are unambiguous: their
presence in a statement almost invariably accompanies the
corresponding policy action. This category draws on the direct
policy action language documented by \citet{lucca2009}.

The second category, \textbf{inflation assessment}, covers the
choice of descriptors that reveals the central bank's assessment
of price developments. Hawkish inflation terms include ``high
inflation,'' ``rising inflation,'' ``price pressures,''
``overheating,'' ``inflation expectations unanchored,'' ``cost
pressures,'' and ``second-round effects.'' Dovish inflation terms
include ``low inflation,'' ``subdued inflation,'' ``disinflation,''
``deflation,'' ``undershooting,'' and ``inflation declined.''
Terms describing inflation relative to the target (``well above''
or ``above target'' (hawkish) versus ``well below'' or ``below
target'' (dovish)) explicitly reference the policy objective.
The inclusion of ``second-round effects'' and ``wage-price spiral''
reflects ECB-specific vocabulary grounded in the ECB's two-pillar
analytical framework \citep{ehrmann2009}.

The third category, \textbf{growth, demand, and employment},
reflects the FOMC's dual mandate with vocabulary covering output
and labour market conditions. Hawkish growth terms include
``strong growth,'' ``robust growth,'' ``rapid growth,'' and
``demand exceeds [supply].'' Dovish growth terms include ``weak
growth,'' ``slower growth,'' ``modest growth,'' ``economic
weakness,'' and ``contraction.'' Employment terms are primarily
dovish (``high unemployment,'' ``rising unemployment,''
``layoffs,'' ``labor market slack''), reflecting that employment
weakness typically supports easing, while employment strength is
captured indirectly through growth and wage terms (``hiring,''
``rising wages''). This asymmetry reflects the FOMC's dual mandate;
ECB communications use fewer employment-specific terms, consistent
with the single mandate focused on price stability
(Section~\ref{sec:inst_ecb_mandate}).

The fourth category, \textbf{residual}, collects policy stance and
commitment language (``sufficiently restrictive,'' ``tightening,''
``easing,'' ``accommodation''), risk assessment vocabulary
(``upside risks'' vs ``downside risks,'' ``tilted to the
downside''), and crisis and unconventional policy language
(``turmoil,'' ``recession,'' ``zero lower bound,'' ``for an
extended period,'' ``fragmentation''). Forward-looking commitment
language (``will respond,'' ``determined to return,'' ``stand
ready'') signals future policy intentions, connecting to the
Odyssean forward guidance concept of \citet{campbell2012}.

\subsection{Scoring Formula and Classification}
\label{sec:meth_scoring}
\label{sec:meth_dict_stemming}
\label{sec:meth_dict_ngrams}
\label{sec:meth_threshold}

All text (both dictionary terms and document content) undergoes
Porter stemming \citep{porter1980} using the \texttt{SnowballC}
package. The Porter algorithm applies a cascade of suffix-stripping
rules that reduce inflected forms to a common root (e.g.,
``raises'' $\to$ ``rais,'' ``tightening'' $\to$ ``tighten,''
``committed'' $\to$ ``commit''). This conflation ensures that the
dictionary matches all morphological variants of each term without
requiring exhaustive enumeration.

Prior to scoring, a custom stop-word list of 44 non-directional
function words is removed from the text. The list comprises
articles (\emph{a, an, the}), copula and auxiliary verbs
(\emph{is, are, was, were, be, been, has, have, do, does, did}),
pronouns (\emph{it, its, our, their, we, they, I, me, my, he, she,
him, her, his}), and safe prepositions and connectors (\emph{of, to,
at, by, with, on, in, for, and, or, but, that, which}). Crucially,
directional words such as \emph{above}, \emph{below}, \emph{further},
\emph{more}, \emph{not}, and \emph{still} are \textbf{deliberately
retained} because they form essential components of stance-bearing
bigrams (e.g., ``above target,'' ``further tightening,'' ``not
raise''). The dictionary phrases are written with these same stop
words already stripped, ensuring alignment between dictionary entries
and text n-grams after identical stop-word removal.

Each document~$i$ is scored at the level of the four semantic
categories. For category $c \in \{P,I,E,R\}$ (Policy, Inflation,
Employment, Residual), let $H^c_i$ count hawkish matches in
category~$c$ across all n-gram types (bigrams, trigrams), and
$D^c_i$ the analogous dovish count. The \textbf{category score}
applies a Laplace-smoothed normalisation inspired by
\citet{gorodnichenko2023}:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:catscore}
\text{score}^c_i \;=\; \frac{H^c_i - D^c_i}{H^c_i + D^c_i + \alpha},
  \qquad c \in \{P,I,E,R\},
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha = 10$ is a Laplace smoothing constant. The smoothing
serves two purposes: it shrinks scores toward zero for documents
with few directional matches, preventing a single match from
producing an extreme score, and it avoids division by zero when
$H^c_i + D^c_i = 0$. A document must accumulate approximately
$\alpha$ directional matches before the score begins to approach
$\pm 1$. Without smoothing, a document with a single hawkish match
and no dovish matches would receive the maximum score of $+1$
despite the weak evidence; with $\alpha = 10$, the same document
receives $1/11 \approx 0.09$. The smoothed score remains bounded in
$[-1,+1]$ and is invariant to document length, making scores
comparable across the substantial length differences between
statements (300--800 words) and press conference transcripts
(3{,}000--6{,}000 words).

The four category scores can enter the regression analysis
individually under the dual-mandate decomposition
(\Cref{sec:meth_dm_decomposition}). Two aggregate measures are
also constructed. The aggregates \textbf{pool hawkish and dovish
counts} across categories before applying the smoothed formula,
rather than summing category scores (which could exceed $[-1,+1]$):
%
\begin{align}
\econscore_i &\;=\;
   \frac{(H^I_i + H^E_i + H^R_i) - (D^I_i + D^E_i + D^R_i)}
        {(H^I_i + H^E_i + H^R_i) + (D^I_i + D^E_i + D^R_i) + \alpha},
   \label{eq:econscore}\\[4pt]
\netscore_i &\;=\;
   \frac{\sum_c H^c_i - \sum_c D^c_i}
        {\sum_c H^c_i + \sum_c D^c_i + \alpha}
   \;=\; \frac{H_i - D_i}{H_i + D_i + \alpha},
   \label{eq:netscore}
\end{align}
%
where $H_i = \sum_c H^c_i$ and $D_i = \sum_c D^c_i$ are the total
hawkish and dovish counts. The denominator normalises by total
directional content plus the smoothing constant rather than by
document length: a document with many hawkish matches and few dovish
matches produces a score approaching $+1$ as directional content
grows. Positive values indicate net hawkish tone; negative values
indicate net dovish tone; values near zero indicate balanced or
neutral communication.

For the agreement analysis and label-based comparisons, the
continuous score is discretised using a symmetric $\pm 0.05$
neutral band:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:classification}
\text{label}_i =
\begin{cases}
\text{\textsc{hawkish}} & \text{if } \netscore_i > +0.05 \\
\text{\textsc{dovish}}  & \text{if } \netscore_i < -0.05 \\
\text{\textsc{neutral}} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
%
The $\pm 0.05$ threshold defines a narrow symmetric neutral band.
Given the Laplace smoothing, a score of $0.05$ requires a modest
imbalance between hawkish and dovish counts (e.g., roughly 11
hawkish versus 10 dovish, depending on total matches). Documents
with near-balanced directional content are classified as neutral
rather than forcing a directional label onto noisy measurements.
For the regression analysis, the continuous \netscore{} is used
directly, and the threshold plays no role.

\subsection{Forward/Backward Sentence Decomposition}
\label{sec:meth_fwd_bwd}

To test whether forward-looking and backward-looking language carry
differential market signals, each document is split at the sentence
level prior to scoring. Sentence boundaries are detected by
splitting on terminal punctuation followed by whitespace.

A sentence is classified as \emph{forward-looking} if it contains
at least one match to a set of 30+ regular expression patterns
representing futurity, conditionality, commitment, or
data-dependence. These patterns fall into four groups, each
grounded in the forward guidance literature.
\emph{Futurity markers} (``will,'' ``expected,'' ``anticipat*,''
``forthcoming,'' ``future,'' ``prospect,'' ``outlook'') indicate
that the sentence describes expected rather than current conditions.
\emph{Commitment language} (``intend,'' ``commit to,'' ``prepared
to,'' ``stand ready,'' ``at least until'') represents Odyssean
forward guidance in the taxonomy of \citet{campbell2012}.
\emph{Projection language} (``project,'' ``forecast,'' ``foresee,''
``envisage,'' ``on track to,'' ``trajectory'') represents Delphic
forward guidance conveying the central bank's forecast.
\emph{Conditionality and data-dependence markers} (``data
dependent,'' ``meeting by meeting,'' ``conditional,'' ``contingent,''
``if warranted,'' ``remains to be seen'') tie future actions to
incoming data, characteristic of recent FOMC and ECB communication.

\Cref{fig:fwbw_taxonomy} presents these four groups together with
their mapping to the \citet{campbell2012} typology.

\subsection{Pipeline Design and Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:meth_rf_design}
\label{sec:meth_rf_tfidf}

As a data-driven complement to the hand-crafted dictionary, a
Random Forest classifier \citep{breiman2001} is trained to predict
the direction of the interest rate decision from the textual
content of each document. The target variable is:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rf_target}
y_i = \text{sign}(\bpschange_i) \in
  \{\text{cut},\; \text{hold},\; \text{hike}\}
\end{equation}

This is a \emph{same-meeting} (descriptive) design: the classifier
predicts the decision that accompanies each document, not a future
decision. Separate models are trained for each of the four corpora,
allowing each classifier to learn institution-specific and
document-type-specific language patterns. Documents with missing
rate decisions, missing text, or text shorter than 100 characters
are excluded. All documents are sorted chronologically before
training. The random seed is set to 42.

The full pipeline is summarised in \Cref{fig:rf_pipeline}; the
remainder of this section details each stage.

The feature matrix is constructed through a six-step pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tokenisation.} Each document is lowercased and
    tokenised into bigrams. Unigrams are excluded based on
    preliminary experiments showing that bigrams capture
    stance-relevant multi-word expressions that isolated unigrams
    miss, consistent with the importance of phrasal expressions
    documented in Section~\ref{sec:meth_dict_ngrams}.

  \item \textbf{Stop-word filtering.} Bigrams where \emph{either}
    word appears in the stop-word list are removed.

  \item \textbf{Stemming.} Both words in each surviving bigram are
    Porter-stemmed.

  \item \textbf{Blocklist filtering.} 94 stemmed bigrams are removed
    to prevent identity leakage (see below).

  \item \textbf{Document frequency filter.} Bigrams appearing in
    fewer than $\text{MIN\_DOC\_FREQ} = 3$ documents are removed.
    The remaining are ranked by document frequency and the top
    $\text{MAX\_FEATURES} = 2{,}000$ retained.

  \item \textbf{TF-IDF weighting.} Term frequency--inverse document
    frequency weighting produces a document-term matrix
    $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ where
    $p \leq 2{,}000$.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Blocklist, Class Balancing, and Feature Selection}
\label{sec:meth_rf_blocklist}
\label{sec:meth_rf_balanced}
\label{sec:meth_lasso}

An underappreciated problem in text classification of institutional
documents is \emph{identity leakage}: features that predict the
outcome through the identity of the speaker or the historical era
rather than through stance-bearing language. Feature-importance
inspection of earlier model versions revealed three categories of
leaking bigrams:

\emph{Institutional boilerplate}: Regional Federal Reserve Bank
name pairs (``cleveland richmond,'' ``kansa citi'') from the
discount-rate voting roll call, and ECB header/footer phrases
(``press confer,'' ``reproduct permit'').

\emph{Personal names}: FOMC Chairs (``alan greenspan,'' ``jerom
powel'') and ECB Presidents (``mario draghi,'' ``christin lagard'')
were classified as hawkish or dovish because each served during
specific rate regimes, not because their names carry stance content.

\emph{Addresses and era markers}: ``washington dc'' and ``constitut
avenu'' (the Federal Reserve's address) appeared in boilerplate
common during certain periods. ``centuri low'' (``century lows'')
appeared only during the low-rate era.

The blocklist removes 94 stemmed bigrams (51 institutional
boilerplate, 43 names/places/era markers) to ensure that the
classifier relies on genuine stance-bearing language. The full
list is reported in \Cref{app:blocklist}.

``Hold'' decisions constitute 60--70\% of meetings. Without
correction, the classifier would achieve high overall accuracy by
defaulting to the majority class \citep{he2009}. The Random Forest
is therefore trained as a \emph{weighted random forest} in the
sense of \citet{chen2004}: each observation receives a case weight
inversely proportional to its class frequency, so that observations
from the rare cut and hike classes are oversampled in the bootstrap
draws of each tree relative to hold observations. Specifically, an
observation in class $k$ receives weight
$w_i \propto 1/n_k$, normalised so $\sum_i w_i = n$. The weights
enter ranger's bootstrap via the \texttt{case.weights} argument,
producing trees that approximate equal class representation
without the rigidity of strict undersampling. The forest comprises
$B = 100$ trees per corpus.

When the number of features (bigrams) approaches or exceeds the
number of documents, overfitting becomes a concern. A multinomial
logistic regression with $L_1$ penalty is fitted to select the most
discriminative features \citep{tibshirani1996, friedman2010}:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lasso}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{Lasso}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
  \left\{
    -\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}; \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y})
    + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \|\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\|_1
  \right\}
\end{equation}
%
The regularisation parameter is set to $\lambda_{\text{1se}}$ (the
largest $\lambda$ within one standard error of the cross-validated
minimum), favouring parsimony. If fewer than 5~features survive at
$\lambda_{\text{1se}}$, $\lambda_{\min}$ is used. If the Lasso
still selects fewer than 5~features at $\lambda_{\min}$, the
pipeline falls back to the 200 most frequent bigrams in the
training set, allowing the Random Forest to perform implicit
feature selection through its splitting criterion. The selected
features are the union of nonzero coefficients across all three
classes, and the class-balanced Random Forest is trained on only
these features.

Model performance is assessed via OOB accuracy, balanced accuracy
(Equation~\ref{eq:balanced_acc}), and chronological holdout
accuracy (80/20 split by date):
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:balanced_acc}
\text{Balanced Accuracy} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K}
  \frac{\text{TP}_k}{\text{TP}_k + \text{FN}_k},
  \qquad K=3.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Score Construction and Feature Importance}
\label{sec:meth_rf_score}
\label{sec:meth_rf_importance}
\label{sec:meth_model_selection}

OOB class probability estimates are used to construct a continuous
score analogous to \netscore{}:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rfscore}
\rfscore_i \;=\; \hat{P}_i(\text{hike}) - \hat{P}_i(\text{cut})
\end{equation}
%
ranging from $-1$ (certain cut) to $+1$ (certain hike). The OOB
probabilities provide an internally cross-validated estimate that
avoids overfitting \citep{breiman2001}. The discrete label is:
$\text{rf\_label}_i = \arg\max_k \hat{P}_i(k)$.

Feature importance is measured by Mean Decrease in Gini impurity
(MDG). Direction is assigned by comparing mean TF-IDF values across
classes:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:direction}
\text{direction}_j =
  \overline{x}_{j|\text{hike}} - \overline{x}_{j|\text{cut}}
\end{equation}
%
Positive direction indicates hawkish; negative indicates dovish.
Top features ranked by $\text{MDG}_j \times |\text{direction}_j|$
form a data-driven dictionary of stance-bearing bigrams.


\subsection{Forecasting Variants and Expanding Window}
\label{sec:meth_rf_rq2}

The same-meeting Random Forest described above produces $\rfscore_i$
for the RQ1 market-reaction analysis. For RQ2 (predicting future
rate decisions and yield changes), additional Random Forests are
trained using an expanding-window procedure that ensures strict
temporal separation between training data and prediction targets.

\paragraph{Forecast targets.}
Four continuous targets are predicted for each corpus:
\texttt{bps\_next} (rate decision at meeting $t{+}1$),
\texttt{bps\_next2} (two meetings ahead),
\texttt{y2\_next} (two-year yield change at $t{+}1$), and
\texttt{y2\_next2} (two meetings ahead). Each target is paired with
an inertia control: \texttt{bps\_curr} for the rate targets and
\texttt{y2\_curr} for the yield targets.

\paragraph{Expanding-window procedure.}
At each meeting $t \geq t_{\min} = 30$, the pipeline: (i)~fits the
TF-IDF vocabulary and inverse document frequency weights on
documents $1, \dots, t{-}1$ only, freezing the training vocabulary;
(ii)~transforms the test document at meeting~$t$ using the frozen
vocabulary and IDF weights, ensuring no information from future
documents leaks into the feature matrix; (iii)~optionally refits
the Lasso feature selection on the training TF-IDF matrix (every
$L = 4$ meetings; the most recently selected feature set is cached
otherwise); (iv)~trains a fresh Random Forest regression on the
Lasso-selected features of documents $1, \dots, t{-}1$; (v)~predicts
the target for document~$t$ and records the prediction.

The result, $\hat{y}^{\text{xw}}_t$, is a genuine out-of-sample
forecast: at no point does the model that generated
$\hat{y}^{\text{xw}}_t$ have access to data at or after meeting~$t$.
The Lasso fallback described above also applies: if the Lasso
returns fewer than 5 features, the top-200 training bigrams are
used. Expanding-window $R^2$ and RMSE are computed on the pooled
out-of-sample predictions and compared against the naive benchmark
(predicting the training-set mean). The performance gap
$R^2_{\text{OOB}} - R^2_{\text{xw}}$ is reported as a transparency
premium, quantifying the degree to which full-sample OOB estimates
overstate genuine forecasting power.

\section{RoBERTa Sentence-Level Classifier}
\label{sec:meth_bert}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

As a third scoring method, a fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier
\citep{shah2023} is applied at the sentence level to every document
in the corpus. Unlike the dictionary, which matches fixed n-gram
patterns, and the Random Forest, which operates on document-level
bigram TF-IDF features, the RoBERTa model processes each sentence
in its full sequential context, capturing negation, qualification,
and syntactic structure that bag-of-words methods cannot represent.

\subsection{Model and Sentence-Level Classification}

The model is the FOMC-RoBERTa classifier of \citet{shah2023},
fine-tuned on approximately 10,000 manually annotated FOMC sentences
and publicly available on HuggingFace
(\texttt{gtfintechlab/FOMC-RoBERTa}). It classifies each sentence
into three classes (hawkish, dovish, neutral) and outputs
probability estimates $\hat{P}(\text{hawk})$,
$\hat{P}(\text{dove})$, and $\hat{P}(\text{neutral})$ summing to
one. The predicted label is assigned by argmax.

Each document in the corpus is split into sentences, and every
sentence is classified independently. The sentence-level
predictions are stored alongside a binary \texttt{is\_forward}
flag from the forward/backward decomposition
(\Cref{sec:meth_fwd_bwd}), enabling separate aggregation over
forward-looking and backward-looking sentences.

\subsection{Document-Level Aggregation}

Sentence-level predictions are aggregated to the document level
using the same Laplace-smoothed formula as the dictionary. Let
$n^H_i$ and $n^D_i$ denote the number of sentences in document~$i$
classified as hawkish and dovish, respectively. The document-level
score is:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bertscore}
\bertscore_i \;=\;
  \frac{n^H_i - n^D_i}{n^H_i + n^D_i + \alpha},
  \qquad \alpha = 10.
\end{equation}
%
This produces a score bounded in $[-1,+1]$ with the same smoothing
behaviour as \netscore{}: documents with few stance-bearing
sentences are shrunk toward zero. The discrete label uses the same
$\pm 0.05$ threshold as the dictionary
(Equation~\ref{eq:classification}).

A forward-only variant, $\bertscore^{\text{fwd}}_i$, is constructed
by restricting the count to sentences where
$\texttt{is\_forward} = \text{TRUE}$.

\subsection{Expanding-Window RoBERTa Forecasts}

The RoBERTa sentence-level classification is performed once on the
full corpus (the model is not retrained). However, for the RQ2
forecasting analysis, the \emph{regression layer} that maps
$\bertscore_i$ to a forecast target is fitted in an
expanding-window design analogous to the Random Forest procedure.
At each meeting $t \geq 30$, an OLS regression of the form
$\text{target}_t = \alpha + \beta \cdot \bertscore_t
  + \gamma \cdot \text{control}_t + \varepsilon_t$
is estimated on documents $1, \dots, t{-}1$, and the fitted
coefficients are used to predict the target at meeting~$t$. The
same four targets and controls are used as in the Random Forest
expanding-window procedure.

\section{Econometric Framework}
\label{sec:meth_regressions}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This section specifies the regression models, the agreement
analysis, and the three-way method comparison framework. All
regressions use OLS with HC1 robust standard errors
\citep{white1980} unless otherwise noted.
\Cref{tab:reg_specs} at the end of this section consolidates all
specifications into a single reference table.


\subsection{Identification Strategy}
\label{sec:meth_identification}

The identifying assumption is that, within the narrow event window,
the central bank announcement is the dominant source of news. The
controlled specification isolates the communication effect by
including the target rate surprise (MP1) as a control, so that the
coefficient on \netscore{} captures the residual communication
effect, corresponding to the path factor of \citet{gurkaynak2005}.

The medium-term rate surprise (MP2) was included as a control in
earlier versions but removed because it is an \emph{outcome} of the
communication: including it would absorb the forward guidance effect
that \netscore{} is intended to capture. All regressions use OLS
with HC1 robust standard errors \citep{white1980}.


\subsection{RQ1: Market Reaction Specifications}
\label{sec:meth_rq1}
\label{sec:meth_expected_signs}
\label{sec:meth_fwd_reg}
\label{sec:meth_dm_decomposition}
\label{sec:meth_rf_reg}
\label{sec:meth_horserace}
\label{sec:meth_fe}

The baseline RQ1 regressions estimate the association between
textual tone and same-day market reactions:
%
\begin{align}
\text{(Naive):}\quad
Y_i &= \alpha + \beta \cdot \netscore_i + \varepsilon_i
   \label{eq:naive}\\[2pt]
\text{(Controlled):}\quad
Y_i &= \alpha + \beta \cdot \netscore_i
    + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i
   \label{eq:controlled}
\end{align}
%
Both are estimated separately for each corpus and each market
outcome. For the ECB, MP1 is replaced by OIS\_MP1.

\paragraph{Expected signs.}
Under the standard monetary policy channel, hawkish tone increases
discount rates, predicting: $\beta < 0$ for equities,
$\beta < 0$ for EUR/USD (FOMC; dollar appreciation) but
$\beta > 0$ for EUR/USD (ECB; euro appreciation), and $\beta > 0$
for yields. The information effect \citep{nakamura2018} may produce
$\beta \geq 0$ for equities if hawkish tone also signals economic
strength. Yield predictions are unambiguous: $\beta > 0$ under both
channels.

\paragraph{Forward/backward decomposition.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fwd_bwd}
Y_i = \alpha
  + \beta_1 \cdot \netscore^{\text{fwd}}_i
  + \beta_2 \cdot \netscore^{\text{curr}}_i
  + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i
\end{equation}
$|\beta_1| > |\beta_2|$ would indicate forward guidance dominance,
consistent with \citet{gurkaynak2005} and \citet{swanson2021}.

\paragraph{Dual-mandate decomposition.}
A core institutional prediction (\Cref{sec:inst_fomc_mandate})
is that the FOMC should react to both inflation and employment
language, while the ECB should react more narrowly to inflation
language. Three nested specifications test this:
%
\begin{align}
\text{(Spec~A):}\quad
Y_i &= \alpha + \beta \cdot \netscore_i
   + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i,
   \label{eq:dm_a}\\[2pt]
\text{(Spec~B):}\quad
Y_i &= \alpha
   + \beta_P \cdot \text{score}^P_i
   + \beta_E \cdot \econscore_i
   + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i,
   \label{eq:dm_b}\\[2pt]
\text{(Spec~C):}\quad
Y_i &= \alpha
   + \beta_P \cdot \text{score}^P_i
   + \beta_I \cdot \text{score}^I_i
   + \beta_E \cdot \text{score}^E_i
   + \beta_R \cdot \text{score}^R_i
   + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i.
   \label{eq:dm_c}
\end{align}
%
Spec~A is the single-aggregate baseline (identical to the
controlled regression of \Cref{eq:controlled}). Spec~B separates
policy-action language ($\text{score}^P$) from economic-conditions
language ($\econscore$), testing whether action and conditions
carry distinct market signals. Spec~C disaggregates economic
conditions into its three sub-components, allowing a direct test
of dual-mandate asymmetry: the prediction is that $\beta_E$ is
significant for FOMC outcomes but not for ECB outcomes, while
$\beta_I$ is significant for both. The three specifications form a
nested ladder, so the marginal $R^2$ gain from A to B identifies
the additional explanatory power of the action--conditions split,
and the further marginal $R^2$ from B to C identifies the value of
resolving the mandate components separately. All three are
estimated by OLS with HC1 robust standard errors and reported
side-by-side for the four canonical outcomes per corpus (equity,
FX, 2Y yield, 10Y yield) in \Cref{ch:results}.

\paragraph{RF, RoBERTa, and horse-race regressions.}
All RQ1 specifications are re-estimated with \rfscore{} and with
\bertscore{} replacing \netscore{}:
%
\begin{align}
Y_i &= \alpha + \beta \cdot \rfscore_i
    + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i
   \label{eq:rf_controlled}\\[2pt]
Y_i &= \alpha + \beta \cdot \bertscore_i
    + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i
   \label{eq:bert_controlled}
\end{align}
%
The horse-race specification includes all three scores:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:horserace}
Y_i = \alpha
  + \beta_1 \cdot \rfscore_i
  + \beta_2 \cdot \netscore_i
  + \beta_3 \cdot \bertscore_i
  + \gamma \cdot \text{MP1}_i + \varepsilon_i
\end{equation}
%
If all three coefficients are significant, the measures are
complementary; if only a subset survives, the surviving measures
subsume the others. This test is applied to equity, FX, and
10-year yield outcomes for each corpus.

\paragraph{Robustness: chair and era fixed effects.}
The RQ1 and horse-race specifications are re-estimated with two
sets of fixed effects to address omitted-variable concerns.
Speaker identity may absorb persistent stylistic patterns
(e.g., Trichet's coded language about ``strong vigilance''),
while the macroeconomic regime may absorb period-specific
relationships between communication and market reactions.

For each outcome, three nested specifications are estimated:
(A)~base specification (no fixed effects, identical to
\Cref{eq:controlled}); (B)~$+$ chair fixed effects (FOMC:
Greenspan, Bernanke, Yellen, Powell; ECB: Duisenberg, Trichet,
Draghi, Lagarde, eight chairs total); (C)~$+$ chair $+$ era fixed
effects, where era takes three levels: \emph{pre-GFC} (before
15~September~2008), \emph{ZLB and QE} (15~September~2008 to
31~December~2021), and \emph{post-ZLB} (from 1~January~2022).
The era cutoffs mark the structural breaks most relevant to the
textual analysis: the Lehman bankruptcy as the onset of the
unconventional-policy era (\Cref{sec:inst_eras}) and the start of
the post-COVID tightening cycle. Combining four chairs per bank
with three eras yields up to seven non-empty fixed-effect cells per
corpus, sufficient for identification while avoiding overfitting.
All FE specifications are estimated by \texttt{fixest::feols} with
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.


\subsection{RQ2: Predicting Near-Term Rate Decisions and Yield Changes}
\label{sec:meth_rq2}
\label{sec:meth_oprobit}

The second research question tests whether forward-looking text
predicts near-term rate decisions beyond policy inertia. The
analysis considers four forecast targets: the rate decision at the
next meeting (\texttt{bps\_next}), two meetings ahead
(\texttt{bps\_next2}), and the corresponding two-year yield changes
(\texttt{y2\_next}, \texttt{y2\_next2}). For the dictionary-based
regressions, three nested specifications progressively add controls
(shown here for \texttt{bps\_next}; analogous specifications are
estimated for each target):
%
\begin{align}
\text{(A):}\quad
\text{bps\_next}_i &= \alpha
  + \beta_1 \netscore^{\text{fwd}}_i
  + \beta_2 \netscore^{\text{curr}}_i
  + \varepsilon_i \label{eq:rq2a} \\[2pt]
\text{(B):}\quad
\text{bps\_next}_i &= \alpha
  + \beta_1 \netscore^{\text{fwd}}_i
  + \beta_2 \netscore^{\text{curr}}_i
  + \gamma_1 \bpschange_i
  + \varepsilon_i \label{eq:rq2b} \\[2pt]
\text{(C):}\quad
\text{bps\_next}_i &= \alpha
  + \beta_1 \netscore^{\text{fwd}}_i
  + \beta_2 \netscore^{\text{curr}}_i
  + \gamma_1 \bpschange_i
  + \gamma_2 \text{bps\_lag1}_i
  + \varepsilon_i \label{eq:rq2c}
\end{align}
%
Specification~(B) controls for the empirical persistence of
policy rates, whether interpreted as deliberate gradual
adjustment by the central bank \citep{clarida2000} or as
spurious inertia generated by persistent factors omitted from
the standard policy rule \citep{rudebusch2006}. If $\beta_1$
remains significant after controlling for the current decision,
forward-looking text provides genuine signals about future
intentions beyond mechanical persistence.

As a robustness check, an ordered probit models the direction of
the next decision:
%
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{dir\_next}_i \leq k) =
  \Phi\bigl(\alpha_k - \mathbf{x}_i' \boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr)
\end{equation}
%
where $\text{dir\_next}_i = \text{sign}(\text{bps\_next}_i) \in
\{-1, 0, +1\}$. This respects the natural ordering and may capture
nonlinear threshold effects that linear models miss.


\subsection{Agreement and Comparison Framework}
\label{sec:meth_agreement}
\label{sec:meth_comparison}

Agreement between a tone label and the concurrent rate decision is
defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:agreement}
\text{Agreement}_i =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if label} = \text{\textsc{hawkish}} \text{ and } \bpschange_i > 0 \\
1 & \text{if label} = \text{\textsc{neutral}} \text{ and } \bpschange_i = 0 \\
1 & \text{if label} = \text{\textsc{dovish}}  \text{ and } \bpschange_i < 0 \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Agreement rates are computed per corpus and broken down by tone
(hawkish/neutral/dovish separately), by FOMC Chair or ECB President,
and by monetary policy era. The same framework applies to
dictionary labels, RF labels, and RoBERTa labels.

In addition, $3 \times 3$ confusion matrices are constructed for
every pair of label sources: dictionary vs RF, dictionary vs
RoBERTa, RF vs RoBERTa, and each method vs the rate decision sign
and vs the monetary policy surprise sign (MP1/OIS\_MP1).

The three-way method comparison is conducted along five dimensions:
(i)~agreement rates with rate decisions, including era-level and
speaker-level breakdowns; (ii)~score distributions, standardised
(z-scored) within each corpus for shape comparison;
(iii)~label cross-tabulation via the confusion matrices described
above; (iv)~pairwise and joint correlations among the three scores;
and (v)~horse-race regressions (Equation~\ref{eq:horserace}).
Together, these assess whether the Random Forest and RoBERTa add
value beyond the dictionary and whether the additional complexity
is justified.
